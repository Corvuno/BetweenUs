# Design — looks, layout, structure

Design is first about use: what someone does with a part, that they understand it, that it is
logical. How it looks serves that, and pretty is what the owner likes. Design goes wrong when
pictures come before understanding: rounds are costly, the options look alike and they are built
on guesses. So understanding comes first, and pictures late.

- **What is settled.** Each design topic has a record of what the owner decided, in their own
  words, what is open, and your proposals (`project.md` says where). Only their words settle
  something. Start every round from it.
- **What it is.** Before anything else, work out what this part is, what it does, why it exists,
  and what someone using it does with it and how: see the product as it runs, every moment the part
  shows up in, its code and the vision. Say it back to the owner in your own words, with what
  someone does at each moment and what matters most there, and settle that before anything is
  drawn.
- **Ask before the first picture.** The first reply on a design topic, and the first reply to
  every new reference, holds no picture: it says what you see, and asks what it does to the
  owner ("what do you like about this?", "what does this do to you?"). Then say back in words
  what you think they are after, and draw only once they agree with the words. A picture made on
  a guess of what they like gets turned down and teaches little; the same guess asked as a
  question is settled in one reply.
- **A sketch.** Outlines only: what goes where and what each piece is. The finished look is
  drawn only on a sketch the owner chose. A sketch can show arrangement and nothing else, so ask
  only about arrangement; an owner asked to judge it on looks will rightly find it bland.
- **References.** This owner's best results came from many references. Where there are none
  that fit, start with a mood board for them to react to, in its own conversation; what they
  pick becomes a reference. What a reference gives is the qualities the owner names in it (how
  lines and space balance, how areas are cut, where the eye is led); built from the product's own
  pieces they become its own, while taking over its objects or its layout makes a copy.
- **Design it yourself**, in the conversation, with one design skill loaded: a round made up
  the way `owner.md` describes, each option shown on every moment and saying what in the product it
  descends from. Design is whole and contextual, not a choice
  between parts: a reaction is about that whole at that moment, and a part turned down here can
  work there. So when the owner names parts they like, the next round brings a version that makes
  those parts work together and also something really different; it is only a combination of
  what they liked when they ask for exactly that. Options that differ only in detail are one
  option. Before showing a round, judge it yourself against today's version and against what the
  owner has said makes any design good for them (`project.md` says where the product keeps it),
  and say the verdict plainly with the round. "Tidier than today" is not better.
  When nothing in the round is better, say so instead of showing it as progress; the owner should
  never have to ask "is this better?".
- **After the pick**, at most one round of variations, then it is built from the chosen design.
- **Stop in time.** The owner tends to keep going and it gets worse. Every round message opens
  with its number for this topic, and the topic's record counts them, so the count can't drift.
  If two rounds hold nothing they enjoy, stop and park the topic with what was learned, until
  there is a new idea or new references. A third round needs the owner to say so, knowing it is
  the third.
- Describe options by what they do and how they feel, never by measurements.
