# Art Deco

Between Us speaks Art Deco: "pretty and elegant, and also bold and out there" (`vision.md`). No
general design skill knows the style, so this one holds it. It has two layers, and keeping them
apart matters:

- **This file is Deco in general**: what the style is, what makes a shape Deco and what only
  looks a bit like it. It lets you judge whether something is good Deco at all, and it gives you
  more to draw from than the owner's favourites.
- **`owner-taste.md` is what the owner likes** within Deco and beyond it, from their four
  moodboards and their own words. Where the two differ, the owner's taste decides what is made;
  this file decides whether it is well made.

A design is good for this product when it is real Deco (this file), is what the owner likes
(`owner-taste.md`), fits the app as it is (`look.md`, `DESIGN-SYSTEM.md`) and serves the
question on the card (`taste.md`).

## Where it comes from

Art Deco is the decorative style of roughly the two decades between the World Wars, named after
the 1925 Paris *Exposition internationale des Arts décoratifs et industriels modernes*. It was the
first style to be modern and luxurious at once: ornament made with the geometry of the machine.

- **Its sources** explain its shapes. The Vienna Secession and Wiener Werkstätte gave it the
  square, the grid and ornament kept inside a frame. Cubism and Futurism gave it faceted planes
  and lines of speed. The Ballets Russes gave it saturated colour on black. The opening of
  Tutankhamun's tomb (1922) gave it the Egyptian vocabulary: wings, the eye, the scarab, the
  lotus, stepped pylons and gold on black. Mesoamerican stepped pyramids gave it the ziggurat.
  The machine gave it the gear, the rivet, chrome and the repeated part.
- **French luxe (the 1920s)**: one-off objects in rare materials. Ruhlmann's furniture in macassar
  ebony and ivory inlay, Lalique and Sabino frosted glass, Edgar Brandt's wrought iron (gates and
  screens of fans, spirals and bars), Jean Dunand's lacquer, Cassandre's posters. Ornament is
  dense, fine and precious.
- **American skyscraper Deco (late 1920s and early 1930s)**: the Chrysler Building's crown of
  stacked sunburst arches, the Empire State and Rockefeller Center. New York's 1916 zoning law
  forced towers to step back as they rose; the setback became the style's signature shape.
  Lobbies, elevator doors and mailboxes carried fans and rays in inlaid wood and metal.
- **Streamline Moderne (the 1930s)**: the Depression and the age of the aeroplane and ocean liner
  strip the ornament back. Horizontal bands, rounded corners, porthole windows, three parallel
  "speed lines", white render and chrome. Bold in form, quiet in decoration.
- **Its materials teach its look**: gold leaf and brass against black lacquer; chrome; frosted and
  pressed glass in stepped fans; burr walnut and other veneers laid as a mirror image about a
  centre line; inlay and marquetry (flat areas of different materials meeting at a hard edge);
  shagreen; terrazzo; enamel. Depth in Deco comes from materials and planes set against each
  other, never from soft light.

## The grammar

These are the moves that make a shape Deco. Each one says how it is built and where to see it in
the references (`.claude/keeper/references/`, file names below).

1. **Symmetry about one axis.** Nearly everything is mirrored left to right about a vertical
   centre, often top to bottom as well. The composition is frontal, like a façade or a door. The
   centre is the most important and usually the richest place. *Seen in:* almost all; clearest in
   `poster-door-sunrise-gold-on-black.jpg`, `egyptian-gold-box-front.jpg`,
   `owner-cabinet-mirrored-grain.jpg`.
2. **The stepped setback (ziggurat).** A shape narrows in steps as it rises, or an outline steps
   in towards the centre. It turns a rectangle into a tower, a frame corner into a staircase, a
   line's end into a bracket. *Seen in:* `poster-tower-arches-gold-on-black.jpg`,
   `sconce-stepped-glass-*.webp`, `streamline-house-stepped-bay.jpg`,
   `gold-frames-hatched-panels-boxes.jpg`, `pattern-fans-and-stepped-frames.jpg`.
3. **Fan, sunburst and sunrise.** Rays spread from one point, usually a half circle sitting on a
   base, so the fan rises. Rays are evenly spaced by angle, often alternating long and short, and
   often held inside an arch or a stepped outline. *Seen in:* `elevator-doors-fan-inlaid-wood.jpg`,
   `gate-sunrise-bars.jpg`, `gates-iron-sunburst.jpg`, `emblems-twelve-fan-shapes.jpg`,
   `sunbursts-in-diamonds-on-teal.jpg`, `fan-scales-green-and-gold.jpg`.
4. **Chevron and zigzag.** Pointed angles stacked or repeated: speed, energy, the stepped pyramid
   seen in outline. *Seen in:* `emblems-fans-and-chevrons.jpg`, `poster-fan-and-chevrons-gold.jpg`,
   `dividers-stepped-bars-and-dots.jpg`.
5. **Lines in bundles, ending on terminals.** Lines come in parallel groups (three is the classic
   number, five and seven too), evenly spaced, often of stepped lengths: the line nearest the
   centre reaches furthest, or the reverse. A line never just stops: it ends on a dot, a ring, a
   small bar, a step or a turn into another line. A bundle can turn a corner together, keeping its
   spacing, like the bands of a streamline building. *Seen in:* all the `dividers-*` sheets,
   `circuit-lines-heart-mark.jpg` (lines ending on square terminals),
   `streamline-house-curved-balconies-bw.jpg`, `streamline-stepped-curves-concrete.jpg`.
6. **Curves are constructed.** Deco curves come from a compass: arcs about fixed centres, nested
   arches, concentric rings, half circles, quarter-circle corners, fan scales (overlapping arcs in
   a brick pattern). A curve has a centre you could point to. Free, flowing curves are the
   exception and come from Streamline (a band rounding a corner) or a single stylised wave. *Seen
   in:* `pattern-arches-gold-line-on-black.jpg`, `arches-rising-gold-on-burgundy.jpg`,
   `fans-and-discs-gold-navy-areas.jpg`, `dividers-sunrise-and-rings-on-navy.jpg`;
   `waves-fine-gold-on-green.jpg` is the one free curve.
7. **Solid next to fine.** A heavy filled shape (a band, a disc, a wedge, a block of colour) sits
   right beside hairlines. The contrast of weights is what makes it bold and elegant at once. Three
   weights is the usual set: a solid, a mid line, a hairline. *Seen in:*
   `poster-arches-black-gold-grey-areas.jpg`, `emblems-fans-and-chevrons.jpg`,
   `egyptian-black-aces-gold-lines.jpg`.
8. **A strong outer shape, fine inner work.** From a distance a Deco piece reads as one clear
   silhouette (a tower, a fan, a door, a frame); close up that silhouette is filled with
   hatching, pinstripes, small repeats. It reads at three distances: the shape across the room,
   the structure at arm's length, the fine work up close. *Seen in:*
   `egyptian-gold-winged-figure-corner.jpg` (hatching in the dark), `panel-fans-and-arches-dense-gold.jpg`,
   `dense-geometric-star-black-white.jpg`.
9. **Frames within frames.** A border is several nested frames, each with its own job: a solid
   outer edge, a line, a band of small repeats, an inner line. Frames are cut and stepped at the
   corners and at the middle of each side, and the cuts get their own small ornament. The middle
   is a window. *Seen in:* `card-frame-gold-lines.jpg`, `portrait-frame-crossing-lines-gold.jpg`,
   `invitation-cards-gold-frames.jpg`, `deck-box-gold-frame-1920s.png`,
   `frames-and-dividers-gold-on-slate.jpg`.
10. **Corners are built.** A Deco corner is cut (a chamfer), stepped, notched with a quarter circle,
    or filled with a small fan; the lines of the frame cross and run past each other at the
    corner instead of meeting in a plain right angle. *Seen in:* `corners-and-elements-sheet.jpg`,
    `dividers-and-corners-gold-on-black.jpg`, `dividers-and-corners-fine-line.jpg`.
11. **Layers and planes.** Shapes stand in front of other shapes: a fan in front of an arch in
    front of a band; planes of different tone overlap with hard edges; lines weave over and under.
    Depth comes from this stacking and from tone (dark, mid, metal), not from blur or light.
    *Seen in:* `fans-and-discs-gold-navy-areas.jpg`, `fan-scales-green-and-gold.jpg`,
    `dividers-layered-diamonds-and-fans.jpg`, `poster-arches-black-gold-grey-areas.jpg`.
12. **Rhythm by rule.** Repeats are even, or change by a rule (lengths step down by one unit,
    spacing halves towards the centre). Nothing is scattered. A pattern is a module repeated on a
    grid, edge to edge. *Seen in:* `pattern-fans-fine-gold-on-navy.jpg`,
    `gate-panel-lattice-gold.jpg`, `cards-dense-gold-patterns.webp`.
13. **Nature, made geometric.** Flowers, leaves, birds, gazelles, fountains and the Egyptian wing
    and eye appear, but drawn with the compass and the ruler: petals as arcs, feathers as stepped
    rows, water as stacked parallels. *Seen in:* `egyptian-gold-eye-wings-deck-edge.jpg`,
    `egyptian-teal-crescents-and-box.jpg`, `door-iron-scrolls-and-flower.jpg`.
14. **Type.** Geometric display capitals, often tall and thin with wide spacing, or with
    contrasting thick and hairline strokes; the type is part of the ornament. This app's faces
    (Poiret One, Federo, Bodoni Moda, Cormorant Garamond, Jost) are in `DESIGN-SYSTEM.md`.
15. **Colour.** Metal on a dark ground is the core: gold or brass on black, navy, petrol, jade or
    burgundy. Colour sits in flat areas with hard edges, metal draws the lines and edges. A
    second colour is used in blocks, not washes. Silver, ivory and cream are the light grounds.

## How it is built

- **Start from the axis and the silhouette.** Decide the centre line and the one strong outer
  shape first; inner work comes last.
- **Draw half, then mirror.** Build on a grid or module (a unit that every spacing and length is
  a multiple of), so steps, bundles and repeats line up.
- **Three weights, chosen once**: solid, line, hairline. Fine lines keep their weight when a
  piece is scaled; lengths and counts change, not stroke weight.
- **Every line ends on something** (dot, ring, bar, step, a join with another line) and every
  curve has a centre.
- **Every mark has a rule**: an angle step, a spacing, a position on the grid. If you cannot say
  why a mark sits where it does, it is not Deco; it is decoration added.
- **Depth from planes**: overlap shapes with hard edges, step the tone from dark to mid to metal,
  let a line pass behind a solid. No blur, no glow, no soft shadow.
- **In SVG**: draw arcs with real centres and radii, mirror with a transform rather than by eye,
  set line caps deliberately (butt for crisp ends against a terminal, round only where the
  terminal is the round), keep strokes at the system weights, and avoid filters.
- **Check at three distances**: thumbnail size (does the silhouette read), screen size (does the
  structure lead the eye to the centre), zoomed in (is there fine work worth finding).

## What is not Deco

These look close and are where Deco attempts usually go wrong:

- **Art Nouveau** (the style before it): whiplash curves, vines, flowing hair, asymmetry,
  organic growth. Scrollwork and acanthus are Nouveau or Baroque. Deco took the same subjects and
  made them geometric.
- **Generic "deco flair"**: thin lines added beside a heading only to flank it, with no
  structure, no terminals and nowhere to lead the eye. It reads as stock, or as made by AI.
- **Clip-art Gatsby**: a fan, a feather and some gold glitter pasted around a thing. Deco
  ornament is built into the structure of the object, not stuck onto it.
- **Ornament thrown around a centre**: a frame with an empty window plus rings plus coloured
  wedges turns into a roulette table or a mirror. Ornament has to make one composition.
- **Scattered sparkle and random texture**: anything placed by chance. Deco is ruled.
- **Soft light**: glows, gradients standing in for material, drop shadows. Deco depth is hard
  edged.
- **Bauhaus and plain modernism**: the same geometry with the ornament taken away. Deco keeps the
  ornament.
- **All one weight**: hairlines everywhere is timid; heavy everywhere loses the elegance. The
  style lives in the contrast.
