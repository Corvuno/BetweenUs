# Taste — what makes something right for the owner

Read before proposing cards, app lines, wording or designs. It says what the owner looks for and
why, so a proposal can be judged the way they would judge it. The examples show what a point means;
they are not a list to avoid or to copy. What the owner says about one design or one card belongs to
that topic and is true there; it comes here only when they say it holds beyond it.

## Looks

Design is whole and contextual. A part works because of what sits around it: change this and that
may start to work, and something turned down in one place can be right in another. So a design is
judged as a whole, on the screen and at the moment it is used, never assembled from parts that were
liked somewhere else. What the owner looks for in every design:

- **Bold.** Confidence: a shape that commits, a line that carries weight and holds the structure,
  colour used fully, a clear order on the screen so the eye knows where to go. Elegant and sure of
  itself, never timid. The first versions of the app were the timid kind: thin, even, everything
  equally quiet. The title, the line under it and the card face now are the bold kind, and the owner
  calls the line and the logo awesome. Bold goes as far as the elegance allows: one step more weight
  on the small words worked; more than that "lost the elegance".
- **Elegant and readable together.** Pretty and clear at once; neither is given up for the other.
- **Lines that lead the eye.** Line work with a direction and a job, that takes the eye somewhere.
  Lines with no purpose but to flank something read as generic ("the standard deco flair for AI").
- **Playfulness.** Something going on, something to discover; never clean, predictable and safe for
  its own sake. A screen where everything is "muted and all over the place" is the opposite.
- **Uniformity.** The same kind of thing behaves the same way everywhere in the app: a fix to one
  hint, one setting or one sheet is a fix to all of its kind ("not only for the slider, for all
  hints... uniformity?!").
- **Centre and symmetry** are the basis of a composition.
- **Art Deco is the language**: its geometry, symmetry and line work, "pretty and elegant, and also
  bold and out there". Egyptian is close and mixes with it. Modern elements are welcome while the
  whole stays bold, and an element may simply be pretty. Every element is made for this deck.
- **References teach, they are not taken.** The owner's references are in `references/`, with what
  they said about each. What to take from them is what they have in common and how the lines work
  together ("Look at what all have in common. Look at the lines, how it works together"), built from
  this deck's own pieces.
- **It is about the questions.** People use the app around a table and hand it to each other;
  controls are found and used without thinking, and never become a concept of their own ("way too
  convoluted… it should be about the questions"). It looks good because people use it, whatever
  else happens to it. A control is as elaborate as what it does: for the You–Us lean the owner loved
  the rail, The Deck's own line on its side with a diamond to slide, and called two overlapping rings
  "really cool, though way too complex for what it does".
- **What they pick, they get.** When the owner names a part they like, it comes back as they saw it.

## Cards and words

- **A story, not a fact.** A good card asks for something that happened, specific, with a personal
  turn in it: "When did you last discover a hidden place in your own town"; "Where in the world did
  you feel most like a foreigner — and not in the way you expected?"
- **Quiet and personal over clever.** "What you believe when no one is asking." A card that invites
  a cheap one-liner or a date-deck answer misses: "Which piece of technology would you happily
  un-invent?", "If you ran your country for a year…".
- **Sex as grown-ups talk about it.** Plain, pretty and still unmistakable: "Undressed, and honest
  about it"; "When did sex last feel like play", where "play" is exactly the word. Juvenile ("caught
  in the act"), childish ("Sex, told in full") and coy ("The unabridged version") all miss.
- **A stranger knows at once what is asked.** Not too explicit, not too cryptic, one question per
  card, concrete rather than abstract: no life audits, no "belonging" as a feeling.
- **Its own words every time.** A device used again and again reads as a machine: the list of three
  with a dash and a twist, the mirror "X, and what X does to you", subject lists lifted from the
  cards. A device is fine when it earns its place, like three with alliteration: "The fond, the
  funny and the faintly ridiculous".
- **Systems over blame.** Neutral and about how things work, never about who is at fault.
- **Play and fun are real subjects**, and a light category stays light: Attract is about attraction,
  not turn-offs or being turned down.
- **The body is lived in, not owned.** Body cards speak to you in your body (moving, dancing, how
  you look and feel); "your body" makes it a thing you own.
- **Roots is about what the past is still doing**, not sensory childhood scenes.
- **Two categories can be two sides of one coin**, visibly: Unwind, "What you do when nothing has to
  be done", against Life.
